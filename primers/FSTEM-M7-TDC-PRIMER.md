## How to Use This Primer {.unnumbered .unlisted}

Every chapter runs the same six parts. A cold open puts one real person at one real moment. The idea explains the concept in plain language. The case lays out the evidence, labeled Documented where a source backs it and Argued where this book is drawing its own conclusion. A graphic specification describes one image worth drawing. An exercise asks you to do one thing this week. A Field Card closes the chapter. What happened. The gap. The fix. Where you could work on it.

Twelve chapters, four parts. Part One asks you to see yourself as an audience. Part Two gives you the vocabulary. Ten forces. Twelve fields. Thirty-six moves. Read it in order once. After that, it works as a reference.

# Part One. You Are the Audience


## Chapter 1. Your Whole Life Ahead

In June 1940 an engineer named Vannevar Bush walked into the White House carrying one sheet of paper. German armies were overrunning France. Through the interwar years the American military and the country's civilian scientists had drifted apart, and the committees meant to connect them had neither money nor authority. \[1\] Bush proposed a new body to put university and industrial scientists to work on defense research. He had prepared answers to hard questions.

He never needed them. The meeting ran less than fifteen minutes. Roosevelt approved the page on the spot and initialed it. By June 27 the National Defense Research Committee existed. \[1\] Within a year its successor office was fusing universities, industry and the armed services into one research instrument. That instrument delivered radar, the proximity fuze and mass-produced penicillin. \[2\]

Bush was fifty years old that summer. Everyone agreed the gap between scientists and soldiers was a problem. Nobody had closed it, because closing it belonged to no one's job. One man took it as his job, and a wall that looked permanent came down on a single page.

You have more time than he did.

### Perspective

Start with arithmetic. A reader who turns twenty in 2026 turns forty-three in 2049. The Chinese Communist Party plans in five-year blocks toward that year, its centennial. \[3\] The same reader reaches sixty-five in 2071. Nearly every official now running the institutions in this book will retire long before then. The contest this book describes plays out inside your working life, and many of the people who settle it will be your age.

The world you inherit also moves faster than the one your parents entered. The parent volume of this primer argues that artificial intelligence strips away the human friction that once slowed every large operation. \[4\] Supply chains, alliances and the rules of trade are being rewritten now, in documents most people never read. That calls for perspective, not fear.

Perspective means seeing the whole board before you choose a square. Most people meet the institutions around them one at a time. A school and a license. A job application and a rule. Each looks fixed from the front. From above, the same institutions look like what they are. People built them at particular times for particular reasons. Anything people built, people can rebuild.

Some of what you run into will make no sense. A rule demands a credential that has nothing to do with the work. A form asks for the same fact four times. A good idea dies because it crosses two departments and neither owns it. The temptation runs toward calling all of it stupid and walking away.

Resist that temptation for a practical reason. Almost none of these barriers came from stupidity. Each one answered a real problem, often a problem nobody alive remembers. The rule outlived its reason, and nobody was assigned to notice. A person who understands why a barrier went up can usually find the way past it. A person who only resents it stays on the wrong side.

The parent volume calls the whole accumulation the Enclosure. The image comes from Texas hog trappers. They never chase the animals. They build a corral in ground the hogs already cross, leave the gate open and put out corn. Over some weeks the pen stops looking like a structure at all. \[5\]

The hogs were not stupid either. They reasoned correctly from the evidence in front of them. The one fact that mattered never entered the frame. Perspective puts that fact in the frame.

### The Case

**Documented.** The parent volume names six panels of the American pen. Two show the pattern plainly. The Federal Register ran 2,620 pages in 1936 and 80,756 pages in 2022. \[6\] Occupational licensing covered about five percent of American workers in the 1950s and covers roughly a quarter now. \[7\]

**Documented.** A third panel lands closest to a young reader. A study of 18,476 research proposals over five years found a consistent penalty on work that crossed disciplinary lines. The penalty fell hardest on young researchers. \[8\] Nobody forbids the crossing. The slope runs the other way.

**Argued.** No adversary built these panels, and no villain maintains them. Most went up to protect the public, and many still do. The trouble lies in the total. Every panel pushes a young person to stay inside one lane, while the problems that matter now run across lanes. The Party runs one campaign across twelve domains at once. \[3\] A generation trained never to look past its own field cannot see that campaign, let alone answer it.

**Documented.** Bush's committee worked because it crossed lanes on purpose. It kept scientists in their own universities and laboratories and paid them through government contracts. It never drafted them into uniform. \[1\] By April 1944 Ford's Willow Run plant was finishing a B-24 bomber every sixty-three minutes. \[9\] Four years separated one page from an arsenal.

**Argued.** Taking charge, in this primer's sense, needs no one's permission. It comes down to three habits.

Own one problem. Pick something real and specific that sits between lanes, where nobody's job description reaches. Bush owned exactly one, the gap between scientists and soldiers.

Learn one field deeply, and learn a second well enough to talk to its people. The lane penalty punishes crossing because few people can do it. That scarcity makes the crossing valuable.

Do the work before anyone asks. A single page, written well and put in front of the right person, moved the country in 1940. Nobody hands out the job of fixing a gap. The person who fixes it usually starts.

None of this calls for contempt toward the people already in charge. The officers, officials and teachers you meet mostly work hard inside lanes they did not design. The parent volume's own finding holds that no one failed. The lanes are the defect. You can respect the people and still refuse to treat the lanes as permanent.

### Field Card

**What happened.** In 1940 one engineer crossed a barrier between scientists and soldiers that everyone had learned to accept. He used one page and a fifteen-minute meeting. The instrument he started helped build the wartime arsenal.

**The gap.** Reasonable people built the barriers a young reader meets, often for reasons that have since expired. Nobody is assigned to notice when a reason expires.

**The fix.** Understand why each barrier went up. Own one problem that sits between lanes, and do the work before anyone asks.

**Where you could work on it.** Any field where two disciplines meet and neither owns the seam. Research administration, policy analysis, engineering management and public service all qualify.

## Chapter 2. Two Men With Nothing

In October 1935, a column of exhausted men reached the hills of northern Shaanxi. They had walked for a year. Serious estimates put eighty to a hundred thousand people at the start and roughly eight thousand at the end. \[1\] Mao Zedong settled at Yan'an that winter with a broken army. No industry. No navy. No air force. No foreign recognition and no money.

Thirteen years later he had China.

His opponent held the cities and the ports, the customs revenue and the recognition of every major power. His army ran several times the size of Mao's, and after 1941 American aid arrived from the largest economy on earth. On paper the contest was never close, right up until the Nationalists boarded boats for Taiwan.

Move the clock forward and the map south. Ho Chi Minh had spent thirty years abroad before he had a country to lead. He worked as a kitchen hand in London and a photograph retoucher in Paris. \[2\] In December 1944 he ordered a former history teacher named Vo Nguyen Giap to assemble a unit of thirty-one men and three women, carrying flintlock rifles. Ten years later that unit destroyed a French army at Dien Bien Phu. \[3\] Twenty years after that it outlasted the most powerful military the world had ever assembled.

Two men, close to nothing. Two of the largest powers on earth on the other side. Both times the side with almost nothing won, and won permanently.

### The Idea

Ideology does not explain it. Communist movements were crushed in Malaya and Greece, in Indonesia and Bolivia. Believing the doctrine did not make anyone win. Terrain and patience do not explain it either. Plenty of movements had rough country and time and lost anyway. Incompetence on the losing side explains even less. American commanders in Vietnam won nearly every battle they fought and lost the war. In 1968 the Tet Offensive was a military catastrophe for Hanoi and the moment the war turned all the same. Two armies stood on that field fighting two different wars, and only one of them knew it.

What both men had was a method. They fought in every domain at once, on a horizon long enough for the combination to compound, under one command measuring everything against one aim. Land reform was an instrument. Propaganda was an instrument. A corrupt garrison officer taking a bribe did the movement's work without the movement paying him for it, and never enlisted in anything. The method needs no volunteers. It needs only enough separate incentives pointed the same way.

Their enemies were formidable inside single domains and could not combine them.

**That sentence is the whole book, and this primer.**

### Ancestors Who Had It

**Argued.** The reader should not hear this as a foreign trick. Americans have run the same method, at the largest scale it has ever run, against opponents who thought the contest was not close either.

Grant and Sherman inherited an officer corps built for parade-ground battles between armies that met, fought and went home. They built something else. A war of blockade, railroads and manufacturing, aimed at an economy as much as an army. The garrison habits of the old service did not vanish by memo. Two generals stopped waiting for the establishment to catch up and fought the war in front of them.

**Documented.** Between 1941 and 1945 the United States fused industry with science. It fused finance with information and culture with arms. That instrument out-produced the Axis powers combined in munitions. \[4\] Pershing built an army of two million almost from nothing in nineteen months. None of it happened because a rulebook changed first. Bush's one page in Chapter 1 is this same habit at the scale of a single meeting.

### Four Boxes

Set two powers against two eras and four boxes appear.

**China then had it.** Mao's movement ran every domain it could reach, on a horizon measured in decades.

**China now has it.** The Party never put the instrument down. It runs twelve domains under one committee, toward one horizon reaching to 2049.

**America then had it.** Grant, Lincoln, Pershing and the wartime generation ran the same method and won with it.

**America now does not.** Not for lack of resources or skill inside any single domain, where American institutions still lead. What is missing is the ability to combine them. Eighteen organizations produce assessments no single authority reconciles. \[5\] One department funds what another forbids. The horizon has shrunk to the election and the budget year.

Three boxes are full. One is empty, and the reader lives in it.

**A final matter, and nothing in this book works without it.** Within a decade of Mao's victory, the Party turned the same method on the Chinese people, in numbers that dwarf what the Nationalists ever managed. The Chinese Communist Party is not China. The Chinese people are this method's first and longest-suffering victims, not its authors.

### Seize Your Rights

The question left over is the hardest one, and it is yours to answer, not a historian's. If Americans had this once, why can they not pick it up again?

The answer is not that the country grew weak or its people changed. The architecture changed by accumulation, one defensible decision at a time. Nobody voted on the sum. A right nobody exercises might as well not exist. The generation that rebuilds the missing box will not wait for permission either, the same way Grant did not wait and Bush did not wait. It will start.

### Exercise

Pick one domain from your own life where effort in one part is undone by another part working against it. School and work qualify. So do a team and a family business. Write down who owns each part. If no one owns the whole, write your name in that space.

### Field Card

**What happened.** Two men with almost nothing defeated two of the largest powers on earth by combining every domain available to them, on a horizon their opponents could not match.

**The gap.** A materially superior side kept every domain separate and lost anyway. The winning side ran one command against one aim.

**The fix.** Find where your own effort runs in separate, uncoordinated pieces, and decide whether anyone is answerable for the whole.

**Where you could work on it.** Any team, family enterprise or organization split across departments that do not talk to each other is this same box, at a smaller scale.

# Part Two. How the Machine Works


## Chapter 3. Ten Forces

Every fight in this book runs on ten forces. They are not weapons and they are not places. They are more like physics. The same ten govern a chess match, a company and a war, whether or not anyone fighting has named them. Name them once and you start seeing them everywhere.

Each one gets a plain explanation, an everyday picture, and one documented example. The examples are Documented. The picture is only a picture, meant to help the idea stick.

**1. Time.** Whoever plans on the longer horizon usually shapes what happens on the shorter one. Picture two people playing chess, one thinking one move ahead and the other thinking ten. The Party plans in five-year blocks toward 2049. \[1\] American power runs on the gap between one election and the next, about two years in practice. Congress has planned far ahead before, at Bretton Woods and at Los Alamos, and let the habit lapse once the crisis passed.

**2. Entropy.** How much force a plan loses on the way from a decision to a result. Picture a message passed down a long line of people, arriving garbled at the far end. The Party locked down an eleven-million-person city inside a single day. \[2\] Eighteen separate American intelligence organizations arguing with each other is entropy in its purest form, a system too slow to move at the speed of one order.

**3. Topology.** The shape power flows through, hub or web. Picture a wheel with spokes against a spiderweb. A wheel gets a message anywhere in one hop. A web is harder to break but slower to coordinate, since no strand answers to any other. Lincoln ran the telegraph office himself to turn a chain of command that took weeks into an exchange that took hours. \[3\]

**4. Asymmetry.** Fighting where you are strong against where the other side is weak, so that small edges compound instead of adding up one at a time. Picture water finding the one low spot in a wall instead of pushing on the whole wall at once. Winfield Scott's 1861 naval blockade and Mao's 1938 doctrine of striking only where the enemy is weak are the identical idea, found independently, seventy-six years and one ocean apart. \[4\]

**5. Agency.** Whether you were ever free to choose, underneath the choice you thought you were making. Picture a hallway with only one door lit. You still walked through it yourself. Roughly a quarter of surveyed American scholars of China report editing their own work out of caution, with no order ever given to do it. \[5\]

**6. Resonance.** Whether every part of a message hits a note the listener already half believes. Picture a tuning fork, silent until a matching pitch sets it vibrating on its own. Eleven of Taiwan's twelve pressured domains point toward one message, so the twelfth lands with the weight of all eleven behind it. \[6\]

**7. Phase.** Whether the blows land together or arrive one at a time, in a sequence the target can answer piece by piece. Picture five people trying to lift a piano, one at a time against all five lifting together. Hong Kong's National Security Law took effect at a single named hour, and every consequence landed in the same moment. \[7\]

**8. Gradient.** The slope that moves behavior with no order ever given. Picture a marble on a tilted table. Nobody pushes it, and it still rolls one way. Access to a market of 1.4 billion buyers, revocable overnight, gets a studio to rewrite its own film before anyone in Beijing has to ask. \[8\]

**9. Criticality.** The single small point where pressure produces a result out of proportion to its size. Picture the one pin that holds a whole shelf of dominoes upright. A near-monopoly on rare-earth processing forced a foreign government to reverse policy within weeks in 2010, after one administrative halt. \[9\]

**10. Coherence.** The master force. Whether every other force adds up or cancels out. Picture a rowing team where every oar pulls on the same beat, against one where each rower keeps their own time. Pearl Harbor aligned a fractured, arguing democracy into one instrument within a single morning, at a level of unity the country has not matched since. \[10\]

### Why the List Stops at Ten

**Argued.** A reader who has followed the news may expect an eleventh force built from artificial intelligence. It does not belong on the list. AI does not add a new force. It removes the human patience that once limited all ten at once, the way a faster processor does not add a new kind of math, it only runs the existing kind faster. Whoever holds the fastest coordination gains the least new advantage from Time and the most from Coherence, because a plan that already adds instead of cancels is the one a fast engine can run furthest.

### Graphic Specification

A wheel with ten spokes, one force per spoke. Each spoke's tip carries its picture in one word. Clock. Static. Wheel. Wedge. Hallway. Tuning fork. Piano. Marble. Pin. Oar. Coherence sits at the hub, since every other spoke depends on it.

### Exercise

Pick any argument, team project or contest you are part of right now. Name which of the ten forces is helping your side and which is working against it. Most people can name one. Naming three is the actual skill this chapter teaches.

### Field Card

**What happened.** Ten forces recur across a chess match, a company and a war, whether or not the people fighting have ever named them.

**The gap.** Most people can feel that a plan is failing without being able to say which force is missing.

**The fix.** Learn the ten by their pictures, not their definitions. A picture is faster to check against a real situation than a definition is.

**Where you could work on it.** Any team you lead or join benefits from someone who can name the missing force out loud, in a sentence, before the meeting ends.

## Chapter 4. Twelve Fields

Ten forces are physics. Twelve fields are where the physics plays out, the actual ground a contest is fought on. This book keeps them in one fixed order. The order itself is a claim. Kinetic force comes first because every reader already understands it, and it is also the field the Party uses least, since the method works better below the threshold where anyone has to shoot.

Each field gets its definition and one documented case, told in three sentences.

**1. Kinetic.** Weapons, ships and planes. The field every reader already pictures when someone says war. The People's Liberation Army flies near-daily patrols into Taiwan's air defense zone. No shot has been fired. Each patrol still shapes what Taipei's politicians believe they can risk saying. \[1\]

**2. Economic.** The goods a country needs to live and fight, turned into leverage. The Party processes most of the world's rare earths. In 2023 it restricted two of them, gallium and germanium, just to prove the valve still turns. \[2\]

**3. Financial.** Money and the pipes it flows through. A legal structure called a variable interest entity routes American retirement savings into companies one part of the government has flagged as a security risk. Another part of the same government keeps those companies listed for sale anyway. \[3\]

**4. Information.** The fight over what counts as a fact. Coordinated fake accounts have been caught posting identical phrasing within fifteen minutes of each other, dressed up as independent opinions. \[4\]

**5. Psychological.** The war on whether you keep resisting. A government abroad can reach a person's family at home to make them go quiet, a tactic this book's Long Arm chapter documents case by case. \[5\]

**6. Legal.** Law written as a weapon first and a shield second. In 2005 the Party passed a law pre-authorizing force against Taiwan years in advance, so that whatever it does later, it can call lawful from the first hour. \[6\]

**7. Association.** Alliances bought, and alliances broken apart. A single government office runs relationships with hundreds of overseas community groups, while loans tied to infrastructure projects buy the silence of the countries that took them. \[7\]

**8. Cyber.** The networks everything else depends on. Hackers have sat quietly inside American water and power systems for months, taking nothing. They use the target's own software and wait for the one day they might be needed to fail all at once. \[8\]

**9. Cultural.** What a whole society ends up admiring. Access to one of the largest movie markets on earth gets a studio to rewrite its own script before anyone abroad has to ask. \[9\]

**10. AI and Cognitive.** The race to see, decide and act before the other side finishes noticing. A detailed model of a population, built from data most of that population never agreed to give, aims every other field on this list at once. \[10\]

**11. Biological.** Harm kept just under the line where anyone calls it war. A supply chain running from Chinese chemical suppliers through cartels killed tens of thousands of Americans in a single year, and the chain was built with enough distance that no single link could be blamed for the whole. \[11\]

**12. Data, Surveillance and Intelligence.** Knowing more about the target than the target knows about itself. One foreign ministry runs spying abroad and policing at home as a single job, while an American law from 1947 still keeps the CIA legally out of the FBI's own domestic lane. \[12\]

### Why the Order Matters

**Argued.** Fields near the bottom of this list get less attention and do more work. A missile is expensive, visible and easy to blame on someone specific. A data broker's quiet sale of a location history is cheap, invisible and hard to trace to any one actor. A field that never makes the news is not a field that is empty. It may be the field carrying the most weight of all twelve.

### Field Card

**The gap.** Public attention clusters on the first three fields and almost never reaches the last three, which is exactly where quiet, compounding damage tends to sit.

**The fix.** Learn to sort a news story into its actual fields rather than the one field a headline names.

**Where you could work on it.** Journalism, policy analysis and any research role that reads primary documents rather than headlines depends on exactly this skill.

## Chapter 5. Thirty-Six Moves

For two thousand years, Chinese strategy has carried a codified set of thirty-six moves. Each is a tested method for winning through deception, indirect pressure, or turning an opponent's own structure against it. Western readers often file them under trivia. That filing is a mistake. The thirty-six name the actual grammar of strategy, the same moves any serious commander reaches for, whatever language he speaks.

They sort into six groups of six, one group for each position a side can hold on the board. A commander reaching for a Group One move believes he already holds the advantage. A commander reaching for Group Six is buying time to survive. Each group gets one anchor move below, and a Western commander who used the identical move without ever reading the Chinese text.

**Group One, Superiority.** Deploy from strength, and win cheaply. The anchor is *Wait at Ease for the Weary Enemy*. Hold the high ground, keep fresh water, and let the enemy climb to you. \[1\] Admiral Nimitz ran the Western version across the Pacific from 1943 onward. Rather than storm the strongest Japanese garrisons, he simply bypassed them, cutting their supply and leaving tens of thousands of defenders stranded on islands the war had already passed by. \[2\]

**Group Two, Confrontation.** Outmaneuver a rival through misdirection. The anchor is *Openly Repair the Gallery Roads, March Secretly Through Chencang*. Let the enemy watch one force rebuild a visible road while the real force slips through an unwatched pass. Montgomery ran the identical trick at El Alamein in October 1942. He built a dummy pipeline aimed south while his real armor massed, disguised, in the north. \[3\]

**Group Three, Attack.** Degrade the enemy's capability before any decisive clash. The anchor is *Capture the Ringleader to Disband the Bandits*. Find the one node that holds a whole network together, and take that instead of fighting the crowd. American codebreakers used the identical logic against Admiral Yamamoto in April 1943, intercepting the one aircraft carrying him rather than trying to grind down the fleet he commanded. \[4\]

**Group Four, Confusion.** Destabilize the ground the enemy stands on. The anchor is *Borrow the Road to Conquer Guo*. Ask passage through a neighbor's land for one stated purpose, then use the road for a second purpose never granted. Britain and the Soviet Union used the identical logic against Iran in August 1941, opening a supply corridor that became an occupation lasting well past the war's end. \[5\]

**Group Five, Control.** From a position of growing strength, take over the host's own institutions from within. The anchor is *Make the Host and the Guest Exchange Places*. Arrive deferential and dependent, and over years gather enough relationships and knowledge to become indispensable. Then set the agenda yourself. The United States played this exact role inside its own alliance with Britain. It arrived in 1942 as the newer, junior partner, and by 1944 it was setting the invasion of Europe's terms over British planners' own preferences. \[6\]

**Group Six, Desperate Situations.** From disadvantage, survive to fight the next round. The anchor is *If All Else Fails, Retreat*, the only one of the thirty-six that names withdrawal as doctrine rather than failure. American forces learned this the hard way at Kasserine Pass in February 1943, their first major defeat against German armor. Command did not read the retreat as the campaign's end. It reorganized and came back weeks later to beat the same army on the same ground. \[7\]

### Why the Grammar Matters

**Argued.** None of these six moves belongs to any one country. Reading them as foreign and exotic is itself a small mistake with a large cost. It lets a reader miss the identical move when a Western general or a Western company uses it in plain sight. The moves are the grammar. **Susceptibility.** How much more this particular audience responds compared to people outside it.

**Accessibility.** The channel that reaches them, whether that is a feed or a friend or a formal announcement.

### The Case

**Documented.** *Protocols and Ports* names a gap directly. American engineers are absent from the rooms in Geneva and Busan where a technology standard gets fixed for a generation of devices, and a travel budget most firms will not fund explains most of the absence. \[2\] **Standards engineering** means sitting in that room for years, arguing one clause at a time, with nobody outside the field ever learning your name.

**Documented.** A crane at an American port once carried a channel nobody had requested, because no inventory existed to check it against. \[3\] **Operational technology security** means being the person who checks, in a field barely twenty years old and short-staffed at almost every port, utility and hospital in the country.

**Documented.** Almost every case in this book depended on someone who could read a primary source in its original language, rather than wait months for a secondhand version. \[4\] **Languages** open doors a policy degree alone does not, especially Mandarin, Cantonese and the diaspora languages this book's case on transnational pressure documents.

**Documented.** A growing number of universities now pay someone specifically titled for **research security**, sitting between a grant office and export-control compliance. \[5\] Done badly, the job becomes exactly the ancestry-based suspicion this book argues against throughout. Done well, it protects both the research and the researcher.

**Documented.** A foreign area officer sits inside **public service**. So does a case officer at a listing agency, and a Hill staffer who understands both a statute and the gap it closes. None of them are paid what the same skill earns in the private sector. \[6\]

**Documented.** Several of this book's own citations trace back to outside researchers working with public records rather than any government office. \[7\] **Open-source intelligence** asks for patience with public records and satellite imagery, and the judgment to know what a pattern shows and what it does not, a field a determined person can enter without waiting for anyone's permission.

**Documented.** A single component, a listing, or an index fund runs through more hands than any one office tracks. \[8\] **Supply-chain analysis** follows that thread, in growing demand in government and well outside it.

### What This Chapter Does Not Ask

It does not ask you to choose one of these seven over an ordinary life, a family, or a career with nothing to do with any gap this book has named. Most readers will do something else entirely, and that is not a failure of anything you have read.

It does not ask you to treat a classmate, a colleague, or a neighbor of Chinese descent as a subject of suspicion. Every chapter that has touched ancestry has said the opposite, and this closing chapter will not reopen that question just to make its own point land harder.

What it asks is narrower. If you were already weighing one path against another, this primer has tried to say plainly what the work involves. The choice, if it comes, gets made with the facts rather than without them.

# Back Matter

## All Twelve Field Cards {.unnumbered}

**Chapter 1. Your Whole Life Ahead**

**What happened.** In 1940 one engineer crossed a barrier between scientists and soldiers that everyone had learned to accept. He used one page and a fifteen-minute meeting. The instrument he started helped build the wartime arsenal.

**The gap.** Reasonable people built the barriers a young reader meets, often for reasons that have since expired. Nobody is assigned to notice when a reason expires.

**The fix.** Understand why each barrier went up. Own one problem that sits between lanes, and do the work before anyone asks.

**Where you could work on it.** Any field where two disciplines meet and neither owns the seam. Research administration, policy analysis, engineering management and public service all qualify.

**Chapter 2. Two Men With Nothing**

**What happened.** Two men with almost nothing defeated two of the largest powers on earth by combining every domain available to them, on a horizon their opponents could not match.

**The gap.** A materially superior side kept every domain separate and lost anyway. The winning side ran one command against one aim.

**The fix.** Find where your own effort runs in separate, uncoordinated pieces, and decide whether anyone is answerable for the whole.

**Where you could work on it.** Any team, family enterprise or organization split across departments that do not talk to each other is this same box, at a smaller scale.

**Chapter 3. Ten Forces**

**What happened.** Ten forces recur across a chess match, a company and a war, whether or not the people fighting have ever named them.

**The gap.** Most people can feel that a plan is failing without being able to say which force is missing.

**The fix.** Learn the ten by their pictures, not their definitions. A picture is faster to check against a real situation than a definition is.

**Where you could work on it.** Any team you lead or join benefits from someone who can name the missing force out loud, in a sentence, before the meeting ends.

**Chapter 4. Twelve Fields**

**What happened.** Twelve fields carry every contest this book documents, in a fixed order that runs from the most visible to the least.

**The gap.** Public attention clusters on the first three fields and almost never reaches the last three, which is exactly where quiet, compounding damage tends to sit.

**The fix.** Learn to sort a news story into its actual fields rather than the one field a headline names.

**Where you could work on it.** Journalism, policy analysis and any research role that reads primary documents rather than headlines depends on exactly this skill.

**Chapter 5. Thirty-Six Moves**

**What happened.** Six groups of six moves each cover every position a side can hold, and Western commanders have used every anchor move without ever reading the source that named it first.

**The gap.** Calling this grammar foreign makes it easy to miss when your own side, or the side you are watching, is already using it.

**The fix.** Learn the six groups by position, not by country. Ask which group a move belongs to before asking who is making it.

**Where you could work on it.** Negotiation, competitive strategy, and any field where reading an opponent's actual position matters more than reading their stated one.

**Chapter 6. Attract**

**What happened.** A shell structure let American capital reach Chinese companies the Party's own law would otherwise have kept foreign owners out of, and turned an entire decade of retirement savings into part of the flow, without a single decision from most of the people whose money it was.

**The gap.** Every private gate in the chain earned a fee for opening and nothing for staying shut. The public gates that might have said no held no mandate for security at all.

**The fix.** A security check at the point a company joins an index, before the money moves rather than after, would give the closed gate the same value the open one already has.

**Where you could work on it.** Financial regulation, index and fund compliance, and any analyst role that reads a prospectus past the first page.

**Chapter 7. Accommodate**

**What happened.** A platform's own design choices and a state's coordinated fake accounts work as one operation, aimed at a population's ability to agree on shared facts, not at any one fact in particular.

**The gap.** No one on the defenders' side owns the question of what a recommendation engine optimizes for, on any platform, the way a state regulator owns that question at home.

**The fix.** Treat the recommendation algorithm itself as a security question subject to independent review, not only the data it runs on. Label coordinated networks publicly rather than silencing the speech itself.

**Where you could work on it.** Platform trust and safety work, open-source research into coordinated networks, and any policy role focused on disclosure rather than censorship.

**Chapter 8. Control**

**What happened.** A government reached a person safely inside another country by mapping and pressuring that person's family back home, using no weapon and no accusation that would hold up in the country where the target lived.

**The gap.** No single American agency owns the job of protecting a target under this kind of pressure. The FBI investigates crimes after they happen. No office is built to prevent the pressure before it starts.

**The fix.** One protective point of contact, in the languages the affected communities actually speak, would give a target somewhere to go before a note gets taped to a door.

**Where you could work on it.** Victim protection work, community liaison roles in law enforcement, and any research role documenting these campaigns as they happen rather than after.

**Chapter 9. Extract**

**What happened.** A state-funded company set out to build an advanced chip plant it could not build on its own knowledge, and recruited the engineers who held that knowledge from a rival firm, two of whom carried files with them when they left.

**The gap.** The instruments that responded moved at wildly different speeds. An administrative order landed in days. The criminal case took years and ended in acquittal.

**The fix.** A registry showing what a critical-technology patent application discloses without foreign protection, paired with faster examination for the applications that matter most, would close some of the gap before a court ever has to.

**Where you could work on it.** Patent examination, research security offices at universities, and export-control compliance all sit exactly on this seam.

**Chapter 10. Consume**

**What happened.** A free, self-governing society of twenty-three million people faces pressure across every field this primer has named. All of it aims at one conclusion, that resistance is not worth the cost, delivered to people the same age as this primer's own reader.

**The gap.** No single office tracks how the pressure across all twelve fields adds up for the people actually living inside it, versus how it looks from outside.

**The fix.** A published, pre-agreed set of responses to named pressure tactics would let allies react in the same days the pressure itself moves in, decided in advance rather than negotiated mid-crisis.

**Where you could work on it.** Foreign area officer and regional specialist tracks, journalism that reports the whole pattern rather than one story at a time, and any language study aimed at reading primary sources directly.

**Chapter 11. Protecting Your Capacity to Decide**

**What happened.** The same analytical tool that a platform or a state uses to study a population can be turned inward, by anyone, to study their own habits and their own relationships.

**The gap.** Most people never learn the five questions at all, so they have no way to notice when someone or something is running them.

**The fix.** Learn the five questions well enough to ask them automatically. Try them on a feed, a friendship, or an offer that seems built specifically for you.

**Where you could work on it.** Any field that studies behavior for a living benefits from a practitioner who has first turned the tool on himself. Marketing. Security. Psychology.

**Chapter 12. The Jobs Nobody Is Doing**

**What happened.** Seven real jobs, done today, answer seven specific gaps this book has documented by name. None of them pays as well as the alternative.

**The gap.** Most careers advertise themselves loudly. These seven mostly do not, so a reader has to already know to look for them.

**The fix.** Name the seven plainly, with a real first step at three different ages, so looking for them stops being the hard part.

**Where you could work on it.** Any of the seven above. The architecture that produced this book's gaps took a century to build, one decision at a time, and it closes the same way. One decision, made by whoever happens to be standing at that gate when the decision comes due.

## Discussion Guide

Three questions per chapter, for a classroom, a JROTC unit or a reading group. None has a single correct answer.

**Chapter 1, Your Whole Life Ahead.** What is one rule in your own school or community that might have outlived the reason it was created? Bush crossed a lane between science and the military. What is a lane in your own life you could learn to cross? Why does the chapter insist that no one who built the Enclosure was stupid or malicious?

**Chapter 2, Two Men With Nothing.** The chapter rules out ideology, terrain and incompetence as explanations for who won. Which explanation surprised you most, and why? Grant and Sherman are read here as men who worked around a slower establishment. Can you think of a modern example of someone doing the same thing? Why does the chapter insist the Chinese Communist Party is not China?

**Chapter 3, Ten Forces.** Pick one force and find an example of it in your own school, team or family that the chapter never mentions. Why does the chapter argue that artificial intelligence is not an eleventh force? Which force do you think is hardest for a democracy to build on purpose, and why?

**Chapter 4, Twelve Fields.** Why might a government prefer to operate in the fields near the bottom of the list rather than the top? Pick a recent news story and sort it into as many of the twelve fields as you can find. Is it possible for a free society to compete in all twelve fields without becoming less free? Why or why not?

**Chapter 5, Thirty-Six Moves.** Why does the chapter insist these thirty-six moves are not culturally Chinese? Pick one Western wartime parallel from the chapter and explain why it counts as the same move. Which of the six groups would you say a persistent bully or a manipulative coworker most often plays from?

**Chapter 6, Attract.** The chapter argues nobody in the chain needs to be called careless or disloyal. Do you agree? Why might an ordinary retirement saver never notice this chapter's whole chain operating underneath their own account? What would a security check at the index level actually need to check for?

**Chapter 7, Accommodate.** What is the difference between a platform arguing a point and a platform shaping what competes for attention? Why does the chapter insist on labels rather than removal as the fix? How would you personally check whether an online agreement is real or manufactured?

**Chapter 8, Control.** Why does the chapter insist the vulnerability here is location, never ancestry? What would you say to a friend who assumed every person of Chinese descent must be loyal to the Party? Why might a target never report this kind of pressure to authorities?

**Chapter 9, Extract.** What is the difference between reading a published patent and stealing a trade secret? The chapter names funding and direction, not ancestry, as what identifies the channel. Why does that distinction matter in practice? If you were offered a research grant that seemed unusually generous, what would you ask before accepting?

**Chapter 10, Consume.** Why might people the actual age of conscription see a policy differently than the general public supporting it? What does it mean that all thirty-six stratagems from Chapter 5 appear in this one case? If you were Taiwanese and nineteen, what would you most want the rest of the world to understand?

**Chapter 11, Protecting Your Capacity to Decide.** Why is a feed that never argues against any fact still a threat to your capacity to decide? What is the difference between healthy trust in a friend and someone running a target audience analysis on you? How would you apply the five questions to an advertisement you saw this week?

**Chapter 12, The Jobs Nobody Is Doing.** Why does the chapter insist skill matters more than credentials for these seven paths? Which of the seven paths would you know the least about walking into cold, and why? The chapter closes by saying the choice could be you or someone else. What would you want that other person to know if it is not you?
